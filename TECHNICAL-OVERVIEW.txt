Pocket Grand Line: technologies, challenges, and solutions

Pocket Grand Line is a browser-based 3D delivery adventure on a small spherical
world with a 60-metre radius. It contains 32 named islands, four Sea King
landmarks, 22 NPCs, and eight jobs containing 20 deliveries. The project combines
procedural world generation, custom movement and routing, story progression,
synthesized audio, and optional multiplayer presence.

The languages and technologies used were:

- TypeScript: the main game code, including terrain, characters, movement,
  navigation, camera, quests, saving, audio, and UI behavior. Strict compiler
  checks help catch incompatible data and incomplete control flow.
- JavaScript with Node.js: build utilities, automated QA scripts, and the
  optional HTTP/WebSocket server. The server uses Node's built-in modules.
- HTML and CSS: the page, loading screen, dialogue, menus, HUD, responsive
  layouts, and touch controls.
- GLSL: shader effects for the ocean, outlines, fog, and camera cutaways,
  integrated with Three.js materials.
- Three.js r186 and WebGL 2: scene management, geometry, materials, lighting,
  animation, and GPU rendering.
- Browser APIs: Web Audio for synthesized sound, Canvas 2D for generated signs
  and textures, localStorage for saves, Pointer Events for input, WebSocket for
  network presence, and BroadcastChannel for communication between local tabs.
- Vite 8, its Rolldown build pipeline, npm, and TypeScript 7: development,
  dependency management, type checking, bundling, and production builds.
- Node's test runner and Playwright Core with Chrome: regression tests and
  browser-based story, movement, layout, and rendering checks.
- Git: checkpoints, source history, and preparation for public sharing.
  PowerShell supports Windows helper scripts; Python was also used for local
  publication audits and backups, outside the game runtime.

The main technical challenges and how we solved them were:

1. Making movement work on a spherical world.

   A flat-world controller cannot keep its usual up direction while walking
   around a planet. Movement, orientation, and the camera must follow the
   surface. We represented positions as unit directions, moved along great
   circles, and used local east/north tangent frames and quaternions to keep
   characters upright. Pole fallbacks and frame-rate-independent smoothing
   helped keep movement and camera behavior stable.

2. Creating recognizable places within a small download.

   Detailed islands, characters, music, and effects could easily require large
   asset downloads. We built the playable world's models from reusable shapes,
   generated signs and posters on canvas, and synthesized music, ambience,
   voices, and effects with Web Audio. Seeded randomness makes generated scenery
   reproducible. Typed content modules keep island and story authoring organized.

3. Keeping narrow paths attached to the terrain.

   Trails could disappear between terrain vertices or bridge over sharp ridges.
   We clipped path patches against the actual rendered ground triangles, with
   rounded joins and spatial buckets to find intersecting triangles efficiently.
   This keeps narrow trails continuous and fitted to the surface. Geometry tests
   cover narrow paths, creases, bends, shoreline clipping, and build cost.

4. Making navigation respect real walking routes.

   A direct direction to a recipient sometimes pointed across deep water or
   through a steep mountain flank. Piers, terraces, stairs, and decks also needed
   usable exit routes. We added approach-node graphs and checked their links
   using the player's slope, step, water, and collision rules. Routes retain
   their next stop to avoid flickering between choices. Reverse Mountain gained
   stair-foot entrances and a summit waypoint; crossing state and recovery rules
   prevent premature direction changes and route walkers back after a slip.

5. Preventing rescue traps and interaction shortcuts.

   An unsafe rescue landing could put the player in deep water and repeatedly
   return them there. We search for reachable-height dry ground or a clear deck
   near the recipient and prevent deep-water positions from becoming recovery
   points. NPC interactions now check vertical reach as well as ground distance,
   so elevated characters and the Golden Bell require the intended access route.
   Dialogue keys are consumed after use, preventing the final line from reopening
   a conversation or triggering a jump. Focus handling also stops Enter from
   unexpectedly activating a previously clicked HUD button.

6. Making a dense miniature world readable.

   Nearby islands, tall scenery, labels, and sails competed for the player's
   attention. We reduced repeated decoration, reserved clear space around paths
   and interaction spots, and checked island footprints and route anchors.
   Island retargeting preserves local coastline shapes when layouts change.
   Camera cutaway shaders reveal the courier behind obstructing scenery; fog and
   outline fading reduce distant clutter. The overview measures label bounds,
   prioritizes the destination, and displays at most six non-overlapping names.

7. Supporting keyboard, mouse, touch, and small screens.

   Touch movement, looking, and pinching initially competed for the same fingers.
   We assigned gestures separate pointer ownership and added a deliberate
   two-finger pinch handoff. Pointer cancellation, lost capture, and window blur
   clear input state. Responsive UI adjustments, portrait camera limits, and
   touch-specific prompts improved phone layouts. Regression tests cover the
   gesture conflicts; phone layouts and gestures were checked through browser
   emulation and synthesized touch events.

8. Recovering progress from damaged or unavailable saves.

   A malformed setting or job index should not destroy otherwise usable progress.
   We validate saved data field by field, repair invalid values, deduplicate
   collections, bound job indices, and reject unsafe object keys. Storage reads
   and writes handle exceptions, including unavailable storage and quota errors.
   Progress and settings are stored as a small versioned JSON save in the browser.

9. Meeting the performance and transfer-size budget.

   Routing fixes pushed the game to about 331.6 KiB gzip against a 330 KiB budget.
   Ordinary JavaScript minification left sizeable GLSL strings untouched, so we
   added a build transform that removes nonessential shader comments and spacing
   while preserving directives, hook markers, and legal notices. A regression
   test checks all 128 installed shader modules. The resulting game transfer is
   326.4 KiB gzip or 270.4 KiB Brotli for HTML, CSS, JavaScript, and the icon.

   Rendering also uses merged static props, instanced flora, shared materials,
   horizon culling, adaptive pixel ratio, and a lighter phone quality mode.
   Reducing rendered terrain detail brought median world construction to about
   614 ms across five measured production loads. These are reference measurements;
   timings vary by device and build. The separate link-preview image is excluded
   from the gameplay transfer measurement because the game does not request it.

10. Keeping multiplayer and deployment lightweight.

    Optional multiplayer shares courier positions and emotes through a small
    Node.js WebSocket relay, with message-size, rate, and connection limits.
    Interpolation smooths incoming positions, and stale peers are removed.
    BroadcastChannel supports same-browser tabs; the game remains playable when
    a network relay is unavailable. This is lightweight social presence, with
    each player's delivery progress saved locally.

    Relative production asset paths let the build run under a hosting subfolder.
    Build-time site URLs provide absolute images for link previews. During QA,
    restarting the production server after a rebuild ensures its cached static
    files match the bundle being tested.

11. Proving that the whole journey is playable.

    Teleporting between recipients verifies story progression but does not
    exercise the intervening terrain. We combined story smoke checks with a bot
    using movement and dialogue controls, real-controller crossing tests, island
    route checks, screenshots, and browser-error monitoring. We also corrected
    a bot obstacle probe that compared future stair blockers with the player's
    current height instead of the upcoming walkable height.

    The full control-driven run completed all 20 deliveries, eight jobs, and the
    ending in about 12 minutes, with no recorded browser errors. It used one
    in-game rescue after that bot steering problem. After correcting the bot,
    the targeted keyboard replay completed the stair crossing without a rescue;
    a new full-story run was not performed after that QA-only correction.

12. Preparing a public repository without exposing private material.

    Internal notes and QA artifacts contained local computer paths, and earlier
    commits contained a personal email address. We preserved private backups
    outside the repository, removed private artifacts from the public files and
    Git history, made helper paths portable, and added ignore rules for local
    credentials, environment files, notes, and generated captures. A fresh-clone
    check and a scan of reachable history found no remaining credential-pattern
    or personal-path findings at the time of cleanup.

The recorded verification results were:

- TypeScript check, production build, and the enforced size budget passed.
- All 21 automated regression tests passed.
- All 28 layout assertions and 229 island route cases passed.
- All 15 crossing/controller checks passed.
- Interaction reach was checked for all 22 NPCs; rescue placement was checked
  for 13 delivery recipients.
- The control-driven full story and the ending completed, subject to the bot
  qualification described above.
